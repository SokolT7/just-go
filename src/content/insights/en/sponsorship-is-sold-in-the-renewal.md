Sponsorship revenue is won at renewal, not at the pitch. The first year is sold on potential. Every year after that is sold on evidence — and most organisations never build the evidence, because the unglamorous half of the job has no owner.

I have spent ten years asking companies for money for events that, in the beginning, nobody had heard of. The pattern is consistent enough that I now treat it as a rule.

## The first yes is the cheap one

A well-built pitch, aimed at the right person, with a clear audience and a credible number, will get a first yes reasonably often. Companies have marketing budgets. They are looking for things to spend them on. A new event with energy behind it is an easy internal story for a marketing manager to tell.

What that manager cannot easily tell is the second-year story: *we spent this last year, and here is what it returned.* If you have not given them that, you have not lost on price. You have lost because they had nothing to defend the line item with.

## What actually has to exist

Three things, none of them exciting:

**Objectives written down before the money moves.** Not "brand visibility." Something a person could verify: reach a defined audience, generate a specific number of qualified conversations, be present at a moment that matters to them. If you cannot say what success looks like, you cannot report on it later.

**A report that arrives without being asked for.** Within two weeks of the event, not two months. Photographs, numbers, a page of what worked and — this is the part that builds trust — one honest paragraph on what did not. A sponsor who reads a report admitting a shortfall believes the rest of the numbers.

**A conversation in month eight.** Not month eleven. Budget cycles close earlier than people expect, and by the time you send a renewal proposal in November, the money is frequently already committed elsewhere. The renewal conversation should happen while there is still budget to move.

## The reframe that changes the pitch

Once you accept that renewals are where the money is, the first pitch changes shape. You stop selling the event and start selling the relationship.

Concretely: offer less in year one than you are tempted to. An oversold first year that underdelivers is worse than a modest one that overdelivers, because year two is priced off the evidence, not off the promise. The sponsor who got slightly more than they expected will take your call. The one who got slightly less will not, regardless of how good the event was.

## Why this transfers directly to B2B sales

Everything above is the same problem as enterprise account management, with the labels changed.

The first contract is sold on a business case. The second is sold on whether anybody internally can prove the first one worked. Most vendors put their best people on new logos and their least experienced on renewals, then act surprised when churn is the number that kills the year.

The renewal is not administration. It is the sale — just one where you already have the evidence, if you bothered to collect it.
