Qualifying hard means asking, early, whether a deal can realistically close: who owns the outcome, what happens if nothing changes, where the money comes from, and what the timetable actually is. Most teams defer those questions because they feel rude. Deferring them is the expensive choice.

## The maths nobody runs

A generous pipeline feels good. It looks like progress in a weekly review, it makes the forecast comfortable, and it postpones the moment anyone has to say a deal is not real.

Then look at the cost. A mid-sized opportunity that dies at month nine has consumed nine months of a salesperson's attention, three or four internal reviews, a solution design, probably a proposal, and the opportunity cost of everything they did not pursue instead. If that deal was never going to close, none of that work was ever going to pay. And it was almost always knowable in week two.

## What "hard" actually means

Hard qualification is not aggression. It is specificity. Four questions, asked plainly, near the beginning:

**Who owns this outcome internally?** Not who is interested — who is accountable if it does not happen. If nobody can be named, the work will not survive contact with the organisation.

**What happens if you do nothing?** The honest answer is frequently "not much." That is not a reason to walk away, but it tells you the deal is competing against inertia rather than against a competitor, and inertia wins more often.

**Where does the budget come from, and is it allocated?** "We would find it" is not a budget. It is an intention, and intentions lose to approved line items every time.

**What has to be true for this to be signed by [date]?** Work backwards from the date the buyer named. If the steps do not fit in the time, the date is fiction, and both of you should know that now.

## Why the prospect benefits more than you do

Here is the part that gets missed. The salesperson who avoids these questions is not being polite — they are borrowing the prospect's time to protect their own forecast.

A buyer who is never going to get this approved has better things to do than sit through your solution workshop. Telling them plainly, in month one, that the project looks unfundable without an executive sponsor is a genuine service. They can go and find one, or they can stop spending time on it. Either outcome is better than the version where you both discover it in month nine.

I have never had a prospect resent a direct question early. I have had plenty resent a proposal that arrived after months of work when the answer was always going to be no.

## The uncomfortable part

If your team qualifies properly, the pipeline shrinks. Sometimes by half.

That is not a problem to be managed — it is the point. A smaller pipeline where every deal is real forecasts accurately, and a team that trusts its own numbers behaves differently. They chase the right deals harder, they walk away sooner, and they stop treating the forecast as a work of fiction everybody politely agrees to.

The discomfort is entirely in the first quarter. After that, nobody wants to go back.
