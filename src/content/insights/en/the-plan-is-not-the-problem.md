When growth stalls, the instinct is to rewrite the strategy. In my experience the strategy was usually fine. What was missing was the first move — the call nobody made, the price nobody raised, the partnership nobody asked for.

This is not a motivational claim. It is an observation about where the evidence usually points once you go looking.

## What a stalled quarter actually looks like

Ask to see the pipeline and the lost-deal notes from the last two quarters. In most stalled companies you find some combination of:

- A price that has not moved in three years, in a market where costs have.
- A target account list where the top twenty names were identified nine months ago and fewer than half have been contacted twice.
- Two or three partnership conversations that started well and were never followed up, because nobody owned them after the first meeting.
- A proposal template that has not been rewritten since it was inherited.

None of these are strategy failures. Every one of them is a first move that did not happen. And each is fixable in weeks, not quarters — which is precisely why they get overlooked, because a fix that cheap does not feel proportionate to a problem that expensive.

## Why rewriting the strategy is so appealing

Because it is comfortable, and it looks like work.

A strategy offsite produces slides, alignment, and the satisfying feeling of having addressed something. Calling twelve accounts that went quiet produces eight non-answers, three polite declines, and possibly one meeting. The second is far more useful and far less pleasant, so organisations reliably choose the first.

There is also a status dimension nobody says out loud. Rewriting the plan is senior work. Making the calls is not. So the senior people do the part that feels senior, and the part that actually moves revenue waits for someone with less authority to find time for it.

## How to tell the difference

Before you touch the strategy, run this test. Take the existing plan and ask, for each of its main assumptions: *has anyone actually tested this with a real buyer in the last ninety days?*

If most answers are no, you do not have a strategy problem. You have an execution problem wearing a strategy problem's clothes, and a new plan will fail in exactly the same way — because the new plan will also go untested.

If most answers are yes, and the results genuinely contradict the plan, then rewrite it. That is a real signal and it is worth acting on.

## The version that works

Pick the three moves you have been postponing. Not thirty. Three.

Give each one a name, an owner and a date. Not a project — a first action, small enough to happen this week. Contact the twenty accounts. Raise the price on new business only and watch what happens. Reopen the two partnership conversations with a specific proposal rather than a check-in.

Then review them weekly, in a meeting that always happens, where the only question is whether the move was made.

That last part is the whole thing. The plan is not the problem. The cadence is.
